A new player wants to know askdnd added things up the way they would. Option A

The math behind each number on the roll card

Each total the card shows is underlined with dots. Pointing at it (or tapping it) opens its parts, each with where it came from, and a line when askdnd's number differs from D&D Beyond's own.

  • Nothing new on the card until you ask, so it never adds clutter.
  • Covers every roll at once: the card draws every number the same way.
  • The Insignia of Claws line is real. Cole's sheet on D&D Beyond says +5 for his Unarmed Strike, and askdnd rolls +6. Without this, a sharp-eyed player sees +6 and thinks askdnd is wrong.

Option B

The same parts on every Table rolls entry

Each roll already keeps what its card knew (its level, Advantage's reasons, riders). It would also keep its parts, so pointing at a total in Table rolls shows them, for anyone who sees the roll, the DM included.

  • Lets the DM check a player's roll after the fact without asking out loud.
  • Kept as rolled: a level-up or a new item later doesn't rewrite old rolls.
  • Rides on A: the same parts, saved with the roll.

Option C

A "Show the math" setting

One switch in Your dice, each person's own. On, the card shows the parts under each number all the time, so nothing needs pointing at. Off, the card is as it is today (and A still works).

  • Could start on for someone new to askdnd and say once, on their first roll, that it can be switched off. Players who already trust it never see it.
  • Makes the card taller by a line per number; the card already reserves its room as it opens, so it still never moves.

Option D

Wild Shape and upcasting said where they change a number

The two places a player most expects askdnd to slip get a short line in plain sight, with no pointing: under each number a form changes on Battle's top row, and in a spell's card when it's cast above its level.

  • Answers the question before it's asked: why 17 when a Brown Bear is AC 11, and why 3d10.
  • The form's lines are already worked out by askdnd (Circle Forms, 3 × Druid level); this only says them.
  • Short enough to sit under a tile; A's breakdown still opens for the rest.

Option E

A "Check my numbers" page per character

Every attack, spell, Wild Shape form, check and save on one page: askdnd's number, D&D Beyond's, where askdnd's comes from, and whether they agree. A difference always says why, or that it's the DM's ruling.

  • For a player's first session, or the DM checking a new character: one look instead of rolling everything.
  • It's the coverage report askdnd already makes for its own tests, written for people.
  • Opened from the character's ⓘ line and from Help; also a natural place for "Report a wrong number", which sends a feedback note.

To pick

Which ones?

They work together; none needs another except B and C, which reuse A's parts. My recommendation, in the order I'd build them:

  1. A and D together: every number can be checked where it's rolled, and the two places most likely to surprise someone explain themselves without asking.
  2. B: small once A exists, and it gives the DM the same view after the fact.
  3. E: the best answer for a new player's first session, and it doubles as the books coverage report's page for people.
  4. C: only if new players don't find A on their own; start it on for new players if so.
